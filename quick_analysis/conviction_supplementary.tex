\section{Supplementary material: conviction-based inference}
\label{sec:conviction_supp}

This appendix collects the material that supports
Section~\ref{sec:conviction} but would obstruct its argument: the complete
tables for all perturbation modes and budgets, the significance tests, the
robustness checks under a different conviction score and a different dataset
selection, the implementation details of each intervention, and the diagnostics
that certify that the pruned graphs remain usable objects.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implementation of the interventions}
\label{sec:supp_impl}

All three interventions operate on a model whose weights are loaded from the
best-validation-epoch checkpoint and never updated. The candidate set is always
a subset of the training nodes, and the evaluation set is always the full test
set, so that the quantity being compared is identical across strategies,
budgets and perturbation modes.

\paragraph{Masking.}
The features of the selected nodes are replaced, either by zero or by the
dataset feature mean, and the graph is left untouched. The mean is computed over
all nodes of the graph; this is an unsupervised statistic and involves no label
information, but we note it explicitly since it does touch test-node features.
Because the candidate pool is restricted to training nodes, no test node is ever
masked, and the exclusion of masked nodes from the evaluation index is therefore
a defensive no-op rather than a load-bearing step.

\paragraph{Gating.}
A per-node multiplier $g \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is applied to the sparse diffusion
Laplacian, scaling every cross-node entry by the gate of the \emph{source} node.
Entries belonging to a diagonal block, that is those for which row and column
belong to the same node, are left at $1$. A gated node therefore continues to
retain its own state, and only what it transmits to its neighbours is
suppressed. In the hard variant used throughout the main text the gate is $0$
for the selected nodes and $1$ elsewhere. In the soft variant the gate varies
continuously,
\[
    g(u) \;=\; g_{\min} + (1 - g_{\min}) \,
    \frac{s(u) - \min_{v} s(v)}{\max_{v} s(v) - \min_{v} s(v)},
    \qquad g_{\min} = 0.1,
\]
where $s$ is the ranking score, and it is applied to the whole training pool at
once. Passing no gate at all is a complete no-op, so the baseline forward pass
is bit-identical to an ordinary evaluation.

\paragraph{Pruning.}
The selected nodes and their incident edges are deleted and the remaining nodes
are relabelled to a contiguous index space. Since the number of nodes and the
edge index are fixed at model construction, a new model is instantiated for the
reduced graph and the \emph{same} frozen parameters are loaded into it; no
parameter is re-estimated. An assertion guarantees that no validation or test
node is ever among the removed set. This mode is by far the most expensive,
because a model must be rebuilt for every evaluated point.

\paragraph{Degree-matched sampling.}
Given the reference set $R$ (the top- or bottom-$k$ by conviction), the control
set is built by greedy nearest-degree matching without replacement: the
candidate pool is $\mathcal{P} \setminus R$, bucketed by degree and shuffled
once; each $u \in R$ then claims an unused node of the same degree, or of the
closest available degree if that bucket is exhausted. This requires
$k \le |\mathcal{P}|/2$, which every budget we use satisfies; the implementation
raises rather than silently degrading if a larger budget is requested.

\paragraph{Tie-breaking and reproducibility.}
Every ranking is broken by a per-node uniform key drawn from a generator seeded
by the training seed and the fold index. Without this, \texttt{argsort} resolves
ties by ascending node index, which systematically favours low-index nodes at
the boundary of a top-$k$ selection; degree is integer-valued and heavily tied,
so this is not a hypothetical concern. On Cornell, for instance, $90\%$ of the
ordering positions of the training pool sit inside a tie group, and the largest
group contains $44$ of $87$ nodes. The randomised strategies draw from a
generator keyed on (dataset, fold, strategy, budget) rather than from a single
running stream, so a given draw is reproducible irrespective of which other
datasets or budgets were run before it in the same job.

\subsection{Aggregation protocol}
\label{sec:supp_agg}

The per-dataset tables store the headline metric already averaged over the ten
folds. The relative drop of Section~\ref{sec:conviction} is therefore a ratio of
fold-averaged quantities,
\[
    \mathrm{drop}\%(s) = 100 \cdot
    \frac{\overline{m}_{\text{baseline}} - \overline{m}_{s}}
         {\overline{m}_{\text{baseline}}},
    \qquad
    \overline{m} = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{f=1}^{10} m_f ,
\]
and not an average of per-fold ratios. The two differ, since
$\mathbb{E}[X/Y] \neq \mathbb{E}[X]/\mathbb{E}[Y]$, but the discrepancy is of
second order in the coefficient of variation of the baseline, which is below
$1\%$ on every dataset, and is further suppressed because baseline and perturbed
metrics are strongly positively correlated across folds. The two definitions
agree to four decimal places, well below the effect sizes discussed.

Aggregation across datasets is an unweighted mean of the per-dataset values, so
each benchmark contributes equally regardless of its size. The reported
dispersion is
\[
    \mathrm{sd} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N-1}\textstyle\sum_i (d_i - \bar d)^2},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{se} = \mathrm{sd}/\sqrt{N},
\]
over the $N$ per-dataset drops $d_i$; it is a \emph{between-dataset} quantity and
deliberately not a fold-level error bar. Since the distributions are
right-skewed, the summary CSVs also record the median, minimum and maximum
across datasets, and the mean rank of each strategy.

All significance statements use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with the dataset
as the pairing unit. With $N=8$ the smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value is
$0.0078$, which is why several entries in Table~\ref{tab:supp_tests} sit exactly
at that value.

\subsection{Robustness checks}
\label{sec:supp_robust}

\paragraph{Alternative conviction score.}
Repeating the entire analysis with \texttt{avg\_z}, the layerwise z-scored
average over the post-diffusion layers, leaves every conclusion intact and in
fact slightly sharpens it. High conviction attains a mean rank of $1.12$ under
both masking variants and $1.38$ under hard gating, against $1.75$, $1.25$ and
$1.25$ respectively for \texttt{last}. At the $30\%$ budget the high-conviction
drop is $3.67\%$ for zero-masking, $3.56\%$ for mean-masking and $4.26\%$ for
gating, compared with $3.27\%$, $3.28\%$ and $4.01\%$ under \texttt{last}. We
report \texttt{last} in the main text because it is the quantity the equilibrium
theory speaks about; \texttt{avg\_z} is the more robust estimator and gives the
same answer. Note that the degree- and confidence-based rows are by construction
identical under the two scores, since they do not depend on the conviction, and
this serves as an internal consistency check on the pipeline.

\paragraph{Including the two small datasets.}
Retaining Cornell and Wisconsin changes the magnitudes but not the ordering.
Under pruning the high-conviction drop becomes $2.22\%$ at the $10\%$ budget and
$4.13\%$ at $30\%$, against $2.70\%$ and $4.93\%$ on the eight-dataset
aggregate, and the mean rank of high conviction degrades from $1.50$ to $1.50$
under pruning and from $1.25$ to $1.70$ under hard gating. The dilution is
expected: on Cornell a $1\%$ budget selects a single node out of a training pool
of $87$, so the corresponding relative drop is essentially a coin flip that
nonetheless enters the average with weight $1/10$.

\paragraph{Dispersion across datasets.}
The distributions are strongly right-skewed. Under pruning at the $30\%$ budget
the high-conviction drop has mean $5.00$, median $3.44$, minimum $1.34$ and
maximum $12.92$ on the ten-dataset aggregate; random removal has mean $1.85$ and
minimum $-0.75$. Negative minima are genuine: on some datasets a small random
perturbation marginally improves the metric, which is within fold noise. This
skew is the reason the main text relies on rank-based statistics and on the
Wilcoxon test rather than on the means alone.

\subsection{Graph integrity under pruning}
\label{sec:supp_prune_diag}

Pruning is the only intervention that changes the graph, so for every
(strategy, budget) pair we also record the fraction of nodes and edges
remaining, the number of connected components, the size of the giant component
and the class balance within each split, before and after. These are written
alongside the results tables and allow the performance drop to be read against
the structural damage that produced it. This matters most for the degree-based
strategies: removing the top-$k\%$ by degree deletes far more edges than
removing the bottom-$k\%$, so part of the damage attributed to a degree
criterion is mechanical rather than informational. The same caveat applies, to a
lesser extent, to the conviction criterion in the general sheaf, where
conviction is only weakly correlated with degree.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{sec:supp_cost}

Masking and gating reuse a single model instance for all evaluated points and
cost one forward pass each. Pruning must instantiate a new model for every
point, because the number of nodes and the edge index are fixed at construction,
and its cost is dominated by graph surgery on the CPU rather than by the forward
pass on the GPU. Empirically the wall-clock time of the pruning sweep is linear
in the number of edges, at approximately $1.14 \pm 0.04$ minutes per thousand
edges, essentially independently of the stalk dimension and of the depth; the
fit has a coefficient of variation of $3.6\%$ across the datasets we measured.
Tolokers, with $1.04$ million directed edges and an average degree of $88$, is
consequently the most expensive configuration in the suite by a wide margin,
requiring on the order of twenty hours for the full forward sweep against
roughly twelve minutes for Cora.
