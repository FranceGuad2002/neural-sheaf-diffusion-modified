\subsection{Conviction-based inference}
\label{sec:conviction}

The Forman profile of Section~\ref{sec:forman_profile} is an observable of the
\emph{operator}: it describes the sheaf Laplacian that the network learns, and
says nothing about the signal that the operator propagates. The Bochner
decomposition, however, also gives us a reading of the dynamics itself. As we
discussed in Section~\ref{sec: boch}, sheaf diffusion can be interpreted as an
opinion dynamics model, in which each node carries an opinion and the
restriction maps encode how opinions are translated between neighbouring points
of view. In this reading the magnitude of a node's representation is not an
incidental quantity: it measures how strongly that node holds its position, and
we therefore call it the node's \emph{conviction}.

The question we ask of this quantity is \emph{operational}, and it is the
contribution of this section: does the conviction identify the nodes on which
the trained network actually relies? We answer it by intervention rather than by
correlation. We freeze a trained network, perturb sets of nodes chosen by their
conviction, and measure the damage, so that a quantity read off the Bochner
decomposition becomes an experimentally verifiable score of node importance. The
configuration we care about here is the normalised general sheaf, which is the
most expressive of the sheaf learners we consider and the one of interest from
the machine learning standpoint.

The same quantity also admits a second, theoretical reading, which we take up at
the end of the section. For a Bochner Laplacian, that is for an unnormalised
bundle sheaf without edge weights, the equilibrium configuration requires all
the stalk norms to coincide (METTERE REF, TEOREMA). The conviction is therefore
not only a score we can put to work, but a quantity for which we have a
theoretical prediction, and whose deviation from that prediction is itself
informative about what sheaf diffusion is doing.

\subsubsection{Methods}

\paragraph{The conviction.}
In the sheaf setting the hidden state of node $u$ at diffusion layer $t$ is not
a vector but a matrix, $X_t[u] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times F}$, where $d$ is the
stalk dimension and $F$ the number of hidden channels.

\begin{definition}[Conviction]
\label{def:conviction}
The \emph{conviction} of node $u$ at layer $t$ is the $L_2$ norm of its stalk,
\[
    c_t(u) \;=\; \bigl\| X_t[u] \bigr\|_{L_2}
    \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{F} \bigl(X_t[u]\bigr)_{ij}^2}
    \;\in\; \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},
\]
and the \emph{conviction vector} is
$\mathbf{c}_t = (c_t(1),\dots,c_t(n)) \in \mathbb{R}^n$.
\end{definition}

We outline immediately a discrepancy with the theory that should be kept in mind
throughout: in the equilibrium result the state of a node is an element of
$\mathbb{R}^d$, whereas here it lives in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times F}$, so the
prediction should be read as a guide rather than as a statement that applies
verbatim. We discuss the scope of the mismatch in the Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_conv_scope}.

Unless stated otherwise we use the conviction at the \textbf{last diffusion
layer}, $\mathbf{c}_L$, taken at the best-validation-epoch checkpoint and
denoted \texttt{last}. As a robustness check we also considered
\texttt{avg\_z}, the layerwise z-scored average over the post-diffusion layers,
under which the conclusions below are unchanged (Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_robust}).

\paragraph{Models and datasets.}
We study two model families. \textbf{GeneralSheaf} with
$\texttt{normalised=True}$ learns unconstrained $d \times d$ restriction maps
and is the configuration of interest from the machine learning standpoint, being
the most expressive of the sheaf learners we consider. \textbf{BundleSheaf} with
$\texttt{normalised=False}$ and no edge weights learns orthogonal restriction
maps through a Householder parametrisation, and is the configuration of interest
from the mathematical standpoint, since its Laplacian is a Bochner Laplacian and
the equilibrium prediction applies to it.

The experimental protocol matches Section~\ref{sec:forman_profile}: the same ten
node classification benchmarks, $10$-fold cross-validation, $500$ epochs with
early stopping at patience $200$, and per-dataset choices of stalk dimension
$d \in \{2,3,5\}$ and depth $L \in \{2,\dots,6\}$. Performance is test accuracy,
and ROC-AUC for Minesweeper, Tolokers and Questions, which are binary and
strongly imbalanced.

\paragraph{Observational analysis.}
Alongside the interventions we compare the conviction against other node
properties, namely degree, betweenness centrality, the model's softmax
confidence and input-gradient saliency, at every training checkpoint and every
diffusion layer, and we measure how far it separates correctly from incorrectly
classified nodes. Since these comparisons support none of the claims of this
section on their own, we report them in the Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_conv_observational}, and use here only the projection we
need: at the best-epoch checkpoint, the conviction plotted against degree, one
panel per diffusion layer, with fold-averaged norms.

\paragraph{From observation to intervention.}
A correlation can only tell us what conviction resembles. To
establish that it identifies nodes the network actually depends on, we move to
an interventional setting. The protocol is deliberately \emph{inference-only}:
we load the frozen best-epoch weights, perturb a selected set of nodes, and
re-evaluate. Nothing is retrained, so any change in performance is attributable
to the perturbation rather than to a different optimisation trajectory.

We use three perturbation modes, acting on different parts of the mechanism.
\textbf{Masking} sets the selected nodes' input features to zero, or replaces
them with the dataset feature mean, leaving the topology untouched, so it probes
the reliance on those nodes' \emph{features}. \textbf{Gating} multiplies their
outgoing messages by zero while leaving their self-term and every other node's
messages intact, so it probes what those nodes \emph{send}. \textbf{Pruning}
removes them together with their incident edges and forward-passes on the
smaller graph. Using three modes is not redundancy: an effect that survives all
three cannot be an artefact of one particular way of perturbing the network. The
implementation of each is given in the Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_impl}.

\paragraph{Selection strategies.}
Candidates are always drawn from the \textbf{training nodes only}, so that the
test set on which we evaluate is identical across every strategy and every
budget. At each budget $k \in \{1\%, 5\%, 10\%, 20\%, 30\%\}$ of the training
pool we compare nine selection strategies: the top and bottom $k\%$ by
conviction, by degree and by prediction confidence; $k$ uniformly random nodes;
and two \emph{degree-matched} controls, in which $k$ nodes are sampled to
reproduce the degree distribution of the high- or low-conviction group while
being otherwise random.

The degree-matched controls are the decisive comparison of this section. Degree
is the obvious competing explanation for any effect we might find, and matching
the degree distribution while randomising the identity of the nodes isolates
exactly the information that conviction carries \emph{beyond} topology.

\paragraph{Decile analysis.}
The budget sweep is cumulative, so it tells us that high conviction matters but
not \emph{how} the damage is distributed across the conviction range. We
therefore also run a decile experiment, in which the training pool is sorted by
a criterion, split into ten equal bins, and \textbf{exactly one bin is perturbed
at a time}. Every bin has the same size, so the ten numbers are directly
comparable, and they answer a sharper question: is conviction a binary high/low
label, or a calibrated ordering along which importance grows smoothly? We run it
for conviction, for confidence and for degree, since the comparison between the
three is the entire point.

\paragraph{Aggregation.}
Every result is expressed as a \emph{relative drop} with respect to that
dataset's own unperturbed baseline, measured as a fraction of the baseline's
headroom above chance, which is $0$ for accuracy and $0.5$ for ROC-AUC, so that
a cell means the same thing on an accuracy dataset and on an AUC one, namely the
percentage of the learnt signal the intervention destroys. We then average over
datasets, each contributing one value, and the quoted uncertainty is the
standard error \emph{across datasets}, which is what tells us whether an effect
would reproduce on a new benchmark; the ten folds of one dataset share a graph
and overlapping splits, so pooling them would report an error bar several times
too small. The protocol is set out in full in the Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_agg}.

We exclude Cornell and Wisconsin from the aggregation, since their training
pools are too small for a percentage budget to be meaningful, so every number we
report is computed over the remaining eight datasets.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{High-conviction nodes are the ones the trained network relies on.}
The central result is Table~\ref{tab:conv_fwd_masking-zero_last}. Removing the
highest-conviction nodes is the most damaging thing one can do at every budget,
and removing the lowest-conviction nodes is the least damaging: zeroing the top
$30\%$ of the training pool by conviction costs $4.55 \pm 1.83\%$ of the learnt
signal, against $0.39 \pm 0.32\%$ for the bottom $30\%$, with random removal at
$1.69 \pm 0.89\%$ between them. The mirror-image statement is the one we would
emphasise for applications: low-conviction nodes are not merely unimportant,
they are demonstrably more dispensable than an arbitrary node would be.

The same ordering holds in all four perturbation modes, high and low conviction
being the two extremes of every table at every budget, with one exception at the
smallest gating budget (Supplementary,
Tables~\ref{tab:conv_fwd_masking-mean_last}--\ref{tab:conv_fwd_pruning_last}).
An effect that survives being probed on the features, on the messages and on the
topology is not an artefact of one particular way of perturbing the network.

The degree-matched control is the comparison we would emphasise. A set of nodes
reproducing the degree distribution of the high-conviction group, but otherwise
random, costs $0.38 \pm 0.32\%$ at the $10\%$ budget of
Table~\ref{tab:conv_fwd_masking-zero_last} against $2.18 \pm 0.70\%$ for the
high-conviction group itself, a factor of nearly six, and the same separation
appears at every budget and in every mode. Conviction is
therefore \textbf{not} a proxy for degree; knowing how connected the nodes are
is not enough, one has to know which nodes they are.

We must be precise about one further comparison. Selecting the top-$k\%$ nodes
\emph{by degree} is also a strong strategy, and although its mean drop is below
that of high conviction in every mode and at every budget, $2.46 \pm 0.98\%$
against $4.55 \pm 1.83\%$ at the $30\%$ budget of
Table~\ref{tab:conv_fwd_masking-zero_last}, the between-dataset spread does
not resolve the gap, and we do not claim that conviction beats degree as a
selection rule at a fixed budget. Under pruning the two are not even on the same
footing, since a degree criterion deletes by construction the largest possible
number of edges, so that part of what it costs is structural rather than
informational; we develop this, with the per-dataset numbers, in the
Supplementary, Section~\ref{sec:supp_prune_diag}.

\paragraph{Conviction grades importance, it does not merely label it.}
The decile experiment sharpens the previous paragraph considerably, and is
reported in Table~\ref{tab:conv_dec_masking-zero_last}. Perturbing a single bin
at a time, the damage caused by the conviction bins is flat and close to zero
across the bottom of the distribution, then rises steadily from the fifth bin
onwards, so that the top decile costs $2.18 \pm 0.70\%$ against
$0.07 \pm 0.07\%$ for the bottom one. The comparison with the other two criteria
is what makes this informative. Confidence produces an almost flat profile, from
$0.35\%$ to $0.92\%$, less than a factor of three across the entire range;
degree grows from $0.38\%$ to $1.06\%$, so from a much higher floor and to a
much lower ceiling.

Conviction therefore has simultaneously the \emph{least} damaging bottom bin and
the \emph{most} damaging top bin of the three criteria, and this holds in all
four perturbation modes without exception (Supplementary,
Tables~\ref{tab:conv_dec_masking-mean_last}--\ref{tab:conv_dec_pruning_last}).
This is what it means for a score to be well calibrated. It is not that
conviction separates an important group from an unimportant one, a binary
distinction that degree also achieves; it is that the position of a node along
the conviction range predicts how much the network depends on it. It is also
where conviction distinguishes itself most clearly from the degree baseline that
competes with it in the fixed-budget experiment.

\paragraph{Conviction is not a restatement of degree, nor of confidence.}
The degree-matched control above already shows that conviction carries something
the degree distribution does not, and we would stress that no correlation could
have shown this, which is why we settle the question by intervention. It is
nonetheless worth recording what the conviction of the general sheaf does
correlate with, and we do so in the Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_conv_observational}. In brief: its correlation with degree
is absent in the untrained network and is built up by training, growing with
depth and with the number of epochs, and it remains moderate rather than
negligible; among the quantities we measure it tracks the model's softmax
confidence more closely than any topological one; and it separates correctly
from incorrectly classified nodes only moderately. It is therefore a quantity of
its own, and this is what makes the interventional question a genuine one rather
than a restatement of something already known.

\paragraph{Furthermore, the same quantity reads the Bochner prediction.}
Everything above concerns the normalised general sheaf, where the question is
what the network relies on. The conviction admits a second reading in the
configuration where the theory speaks, namely the unnormalised bundle sheaf
without edge weights, whose Laplacian is a Bochner Laplacian. There the
equilibrium configuration requires the conviction to be \emph{the same at every
node} (METTERE REF, TEOREMA), which is a prediction we can check directly on the
trained network.

The check fails. Figure~\ref{fig: RM_Bundle_Scatter} shows the conviction of the
Roman Empire dataset layer by layer: at every one of them the norms remain
spread over a wide range, and the nodes retain markedly different magnitudes.
The conviction is not constant, and the system is therefore \textbf{not at
equilibrium}.

The consequence follows in three steps. The conviction is not constant, so the
diffusion has not reached the equilibrium its own operator prescribes. If a
fixed kernel governed the process it would converge to that equilibrium, and
that convergence is exactly what we do not observe.
The description of sheaf diffusion as the action of a kernel is therefore
\textbf{not sufficient} to account for what the trained network does. What
remains is the reading that already motivated the Forman profile: the sheaf is
re-estimated at every layer from the current features, so the operator itself
changes as the signal moves through it, and the process is a \textbf{geometric
flow} rather than a diffusion with a fixed generator. A flow has no reason to
settle at the equilibrium of any one of its instantaneous operators, which is
why the narrative according to which representations converge to a common value
does not describe these networks.

What replaces the equilibrium is a topological ordering, the conviction becoming
increasingly correlated with degree as the diffusion proceeds, from $-0.116$ at
$X_0$ to $0.652$ at the last layer in Figure~\ref{fig: RM_Bundle_Scatter}. We
would stress that this ordering is not itself a product of learning, since it is
already present in the untrained network (Supplementary,
Section~\ref{sec:supp_conv_observational}), as one should expect of an
unnormalised Laplacian whose operator carries the degree in its own scale. It is
the failure of the norms to equalise, not their correlation with degree, that
carries the argument above.

One caveat bears on how strongly this should be read, and we discuss it in the
Supplementary, Section~\ref{sec:supp_conv_scope}: our states live in
$\mathbb{R}^{d\times F}$ rather than in $\mathbb{R}^d$, so the equilibrium
theorem does not apply verbatim. The mismatch is in the harmless direction for a
negative conclusion, since a richer state space gives the process more room to
depart from the prediction, not less.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

The experiments of this section change the status of the conviction. It enters
as a descriptive quantity, read off the Bochner decomposition and constrained by
an equilibrium theorem; it leaves as an \textbf{operational score of node
importance}, whose predictions about which nodes matter are verified by
intervention on a trained network.

We would state the conclusion conservatively, because the conservative version is
the one the data supports and it is already a strong claim. High-conviction nodes
are those on which the learnt sheaf diffusion relies most, and low-conviction
nodes are correspondingly \emph{dispensable} for the already trained model. What
we do \emph{not} claim is that removing low-conviction nodes improves the model:
individual masking curves suggested as much, but the effect does not survive
aggregation across datasets. The honest statement is that low-conviction removal
is the least destructive intervention available, not that it is beneficial, and
it is that statement which would justify using conviction for interpretability,
for robustness analysis, or for compressing a trained model.

Two properties make the result more than an accuracy comparison. The first is its
robustness across perturbation modes, which act respectively on the features, on
the messages and on the topology, and agree on the ordering of the strategies.
The second is the degree-matched control, which rules out the most natural
deflationary explanation. The honest limitation is the comparison with degree as
a selection rule: at a fixed budget the two cannot be separated by the
between-dataset spread, and what distinguishes them is the decile experiment
rather than the sweep. The value of conviction, on this evidence, is that it is
\emph{calibrated}, not that it is the sharpest way of finding the single most
important node.

Finally, the reading of the bundle sheaf with which we closed the Results
connects back to Section~\ref{sec:forman_profile}, and the two support the same
conclusion from opposite directions. There we found that the learnt Laplacian is
not the object the static theory would predict; here we find that the signal it
propagates does not reach the equilibrium that theory would predict either. Both
follow from the same fact: the sheaf is re-estimated at every layer, so the
network deforms its own geometry as it diffuses. That the resulting
out-of-equilibrium conviction turns out to be a useful importance score is the
most interesting aspect of the result, since it suggests that what the static
theory treats as a failure to converge is where the useful structure resides.
