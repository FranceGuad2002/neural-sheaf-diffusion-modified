\section{Empirical Contributions}
\label{sec:empirical_contributions}

\subsection{Forman Profile diagnostic}
\label{sec:forman_profile}


The theory currently available for Sheaf Neural Networks is of two kinds.
Convergence results describe sheaf diffusion as a dynamical system and characterize its equilibria, while the Universal Approximation for Sheaves proved in ~\cite{bodnar2022neural} establishes that a Neural Network could learn any sheaf that is defined on a graph.
In this work we would like to go beyond these two theoretical paradigms.
On one hand, since the sheaf is learnt at each layer via the update rule $\restrv{v}{e}^{(\ell)} = \varsigma(V[x_v^{(\ell)} \,\|\, x_u^{(\ell)}])$, the Sheaf Neural mechanism could be interpreted as a geometric flow, where the Laplacian changes according to the local data (the attributes in this setting, or the metric in differential geometry) defined on the graph.
Moreover, to our knowledge the ``navigability'' of the sheaf space is still underexplored, i.e.\ how the sheaf learning mechanism navigates the space of possible sheaves and whether different learning mechanisms induce different learning biases that constrain the learning process into sub-trajectories of the space.
To address both issues, it is necessary to study the properties of the Laplacians that are learnt by each sheaf mechanism.
To this aim we introduce an observable: a quantity that can be computed on the sheaf
while it is being learnt, layer by layer and epoch by epoch, and that is cheap
enough to be tracked along an entire training run. We propose the \emph{Forman
profile}, read from the Bochner decomposition of cellular sheaves in Section~\ref{sec:bochner_matrices},
as such a diagnostic, and we use it to compare the sheaves produced by
different learning pipelines.

\subsubsection{Methods}
\paragraph{The Forman profile.}
The Bochner decomposition of a sheaf Laplacian
$L_{\mathcal{F}} = B_{\mathcal{F}} + F_{\mathcal{F}}$ produces a
block-diagonal curvature term whose block at node $u$ is, by
\eqref{eq:sheaf_forman}, an endomorphism
$(F_{\mathcal{F}})_{uu} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ of the stalk at $u$
depending only on the restriction maps of the edges incident to $u$. Since this
block is a matrix rather than a scalar, we summarise it by its spectrum.

\begin{definition}[Forman profile]
\label{def:forman_profile}
Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a sheaf with $d$-dimensional stalks on a graph with $n$
nodes. For each node $u$ let $\lambda_1(u) \leq \dots \leq \lambda_d(u)$ be the
eigenvalues of the curvature block $(F_{\mathcal{F}})_{uu}$. The \emph{Forman
profile} of $\mathcal{F}$ is the matrix
\[
  f(\mathcal{F}) \;=\; \bigl(\lambda_k(u)\bigr)_{u=1,\dots,n;\; k=1,\dots,d}
  \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{n\times d},
\]
whose $k$-th column $f_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the distribution of the $k$-th
Forman term eigenvalue across nodes.
\end{definition}

Extracting it from a trained model requires only taking the sheaf Laplacian
produced at layer $\ell$ by the learnt restriction maps, applying
Definition~\ref{def:block_bochner} to isolate the curvature term, and
diagonalising each of its $n$ diagonal blocks. 

This diagnostic is defined for any sheaf Laplacian, so it can be compared across learning mechanisms.
Moreover, it is local, as it uses only the information within a node's neighbourhood, and it is cheap, requiring $n$ eigendecompositions of size $d$ rather than one of size $nd$.
Nonetheless, the results proved in Section~\ref{sec:bochner} show that it is related to global properties of the complex.
It is instructive to compare it with the spectral decomposition:
computing the Forman term is considerably cheaper, particularly for large graphs; moreover, as we discussed above, spectral theory for sheaves is still a developing field, and, as a consequence, spectral quantities and eigenvectors still lack the interpretability that the Forman term can instead provide.
A profile is informative only against a baseline, and we use two. The
\emph{topological reference} $f^{\mathrm{top}}$ is the Forman profile of the
augmented normalised graph Laplacian, that is, of the trivial sheaf on the same
graph; it depends on the graph alone, involves no learnt parameter, and is
computed once per fold. The \emph{zero reference} is the profile of the
unnormalised graph Laplacian, which vanishes identically as recalled in
Section~\ref{sec:bochner_decomposition}.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
Every configuration follows the same loop: we train the network on the node
classification task, and at selected epochs we extract, for each layer, the
Forman profile of the sheaf Laplacian currently produced by the restriction
maps and compare it to the reference profiles. The sheaf is therefore observed
while it is being learnt, and not only at convergence.

We study two model families. In \textbf{GeneralSheaf} a neural learner maps each
edge to an unconstrained $d\times d$ restriction map,
$\restrv{v}{e}^{(\ell)} = \varsigma(V[x_v^{(\ell)} \,\|\, x_u^{(\ell)}])$. In
\textbf{JointSheafParams} ~\cite{caralt2024jointdiffusionprocessesinductive} node signals and
restriction maps are diffused jointly at each layer via the update $\begin{cases}
X(t+1) = X(t) - \sigma\big((\mathbf{I}_{nd} - \alpha \Delta_{\mathcal{F}}(t))(I_n \otimes W_1(t))XW_2(t)\big) \\
\mathcal{F}^T(t+1) = \mathcal{F}^T(t) - \sigma\big((\mathbf{I}_{2md} - \beta \Delta_{X(t)})(I_n \otimes W_1^T(t))\mathcal{F}^TW_2^T(t)\big)
\end{cases}$. For this process we consider the
identity initialisation $\mathcal{F}^{(0)} = I_d$; in this case the Forman profile also measures how far the
joint process drifts from the trivial sheaf. 
Each model is trained independently under both
normalisation regimes, namely with
$\Delta_{\mathcal{F}} = D^{-1/2}L_{\mathcal{F}}D^{-1/2}$ and
with the raw $L_{\mathcal{F}}$.
We outline that a normalised sheaf Laplacian can be seen as the standard sheaf Laplacian of another sheaf.
As a consequence, all our experiments lie within the same sheaf space, and the comparison between them is fair.
Experiments are run on ten node classification benchmarks: Amazon Ratings,
Citeseer, Cora, Cornell, Minesweeper, Pubmed, Questions, Roman Empire, Tolokers
and Wisconsin  ~\cite{sen2008collective,tang2009social,pei2020geomgcn,platonov2023critical}.
%The selection follows the critical evaluation of (METTERE REF: Platonov et al., 2024), which flags Chameleon, Squirrel and Texas for duplicate nodes and severe class imbalance; we exclude those. 
Performance is reported as
test accuracy, and as AUC-ROC for Minesweeper, Tolokers and Questions. We use $10$-fold
cross-validation with $500$ epochs and early stopping with patience $200$, and
average every metric over folds.
Stalk dimension and depth are chosen per
dataset, with $d \in \{2,3,5\}$ and $L \in \{2,\dots,6\}$: small datasets such
as Cornell and Wisconsin use $d=2$ and $L=2$, larger ones up to $d=5$ and $L=6$.
Profiles are saved at epochs $\{0,1,5,15,50,200\}$ and in the last
epoch, for every fold and every layer.
Doing the checkpoint analysis allows us to understand how the sheaf changes during the learning process, in a task-driven way, since epoch $0$ is an untrained sheaf that is randomly initialised and therefore acts as a control.

\paragraph{Alignment metrics.}
All metrics are computed per eigenvalue term, per layer, per checkpoint and per fold,
and then \textbf{averaged over eigenvalues and folds}, so that each configuration yields one
scalar per (checkpoint, layer) cell; we display these as heatmaps whose rows are
checkpoints and whose columns are layers.
For normalised runs we compare each
$k$ eigenvalue Forman term $f_k$ to $f^{\mathrm{top}}$ using Spearman $\rho$ and Pearson $r$ for
node-level rank and linear correlation, the $1$-Wasserstein distance $W_1$
between the two empirical distributions, the RMSE between raw values, and the
NRMSE, that is the same $\ell_2$ distance computed after z-scoring both
profiles.
We would outline that RMSE and NRMSE differ only by
the z-scoring, so RMSE sees location and scale whereas NRMSE does not, and a
disagreement between them localises the discrepancy in the mean or the scale
rather than in the shape.
For unnormalised runs the natural baseline is ambiguous, since the unnormalised
graph Laplacian has identically zero profile, and we therefore measure against
both references. We report $W_1(f_k, f_k^{\mathrm{top}})$ and $W(f_k,\mathbf{0})$
for the distributional distance to the topological and to the flat reference,
Pearson $r$ and NRMSE against $f_k^{\mathrm{top}}$ for shape alignment
independent of scale, and the RMSE against $\mathbf{0}$, which is simply the
root-mean-square magnitude of the learnt profile itself.

\paragraph{Global summary.}
Alongside the per-dataset heatmaps (Supplementary, Section~\ref{sec:forman_supplementary}), we report the Wasserstein distance as a single global statistic per checkpoint: for each of the ten datasets we average over folds and eigenvalues as above, and further average across datasets. Since depth $L$ differs across datasets, we bin each network's layers by relative depth, first, middle or last layer, rather than by absolute layer index, and report mean $\pm$ standard error across datasets.
When a depth bin spans more than one actual layer, for instance the middle bin of a six-layer network, those layers are averaged together with the ten folds into a single number per dataset, exactly as the eigenvalue channels already are; only after this do we average across datasets to obtain the value shown in a cell. The uncertainty reported alongside it is likewise computed across datasets, and not by pooling the underlying fold-and-layer measurements: since the folds and the layers within a bin all come from the same trained model on the same graph, they are correlated re-measurements of one quantity rather than independent samples, and pooling them directly would understate the true uncertainty. 

\paragraph{Distinguishing normalisation and training effects.}

To really disentangle the effect of the learning process from that of the
normalisation, we recompute, for GeneralSheaf and at every
checkpoint, a second Laplacian built from the \emph{same learnt restriction
maps} but with the opposite normalisation, which we call the \emph{other
Laplacian}. This yields four profiles per dataset.

\begin{tabular}{lcc}

 & \textbf{Evaluated unnormalised} $L_{\mathcal{F}}$ \\
\midrule
Maps trained \texttt{norm=True}  & primary run    & other Laplacian \\
Maps trained \texttt{norm=False} & other Laplacian & primary run     \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each column of the table fixes the normalisation used at evaluation, and varies
only the regime under which the maps were trained. Comparing the two entries of
a single column therefore isolates the role of the training regime rather than
that of the normalisation.

Moreover, to obtain a finer picture of how normalisation and training interact,
we perform two analyses. First, we compare each \emph{other Laplacian} against
the reference profiles, using the same metric sets as for the primary runs.
Second, we cross-compare the two Laplacians lying in the same column. That is,
we train once with \texttt{norm=True} and once with \texttt{norm=False}, and we
compute the distance metrics between the primary Laplacian of the first run and
the \emph{other Laplacian} of the second. In other words, we compute the
differences between the Forman profiles of the two Laplacians sitting in the
same column of the table above.


\subsubsection{Results}
\paragraph{Normalised sheaf Laplacians align with the graph Laplacian.}
Our most striking result is that, across the majority of datasets, the Forman profile of the learnt normalised sheaf Laplacian aligns with the topological reference, that is, with the profile of the normalised graph Laplacian. The alignment is robust across the Spearman, Pearson, Wasserstein and NRMSE metrics.
The alignment strengthens along both axes of the heatmap,
increasing with the number of epochs and, at fixed epoch, with depth, so that
the deepest layers are the most aligned and the heatmaps display a
characteristic triangular pattern, as we show in Table~\ref{tab:global_general_norm} and, for a single dataset, in Figure~\ref{fig: RM_Norm} (Supplementary).
Of the five metrics, the Wasserstein distance
is the most stable across datasets, stalk dimensions and depths.
The exception
is the RMSE, which behaves differently from the NRMSE; since the two differ only
by the z-scoring, we read this as evidence that the learnt profile and the
topological one agree in shape but differ by a mean offset or a scale factor.
This could also mean that the RMSE is not a good metric for this kind of comparison.
It is worth observing that this behaviour is task-driven and manifests during learning.
Indeed, at epoch $0$, where the learnt sheaf is random, the alignment is absent, and it develops as training proceeds.
Normalisation alone therefore does not produce it, and the behaviour is better read as an \textbf{implicit bias} of the neural mechanism that learns the sheaf.
\newline 
A clear exception to this pattern is Minesweeper: at no layer or epoch does its
Forman profile show an appreciable alignment with the topological reference,
across any of the five metrics. Tolokers and Questions depart from the pattern
as well, though less markedly. In none of these cases is the departure
accompanied by a corresponding drop in task performance, since the models still
attain good AUC-ROC scores. This dissociation between predictive performance
and curvature alignment is itself informative: it shows that the implicit bias
we describe is not a prerequisite for the sheaf to solve the task well, but a
tendency of the learning dynamics that some datasets do not follow. We return
to this point in the Discussion paragraph.

\paragraph{Unnormalised sheaf Laplacians do not align.}
When the Laplacian is left unnormalised, the learnt profile matches neither the
topological reference nor the zero reference.
Interestingly, the metrics with respect to the topological reference are almost constant with the layers and epochs. 
Regarding the zero reference, the disalignment is generally very high.
It usually increases with the layers and epochs; however, in large heterophilic datasets the peak disalignment is observed at middle layers and epochs, and then decreases slightly towards the final ones, while retaining a considerable magnitude.
We do not have a clear explanation for this, but we hypothesise that, since these datasets require non-trivial sheaves to capture the heterophily, during the learning process the learnt sheaf may be pushed too far, possibly also due to numerical instabilities, towards a complete disalignment phase, and then be corrected towards milder values in the final layers.
The global pattern is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:global_general_unnorm}; an example of a typical unnormalised behaviour for a single dataset is shown in Figure~\ref{fig: Citeseer_Not_Norm} (Supplementary).

\paragraph{Alignment tracks both normalization and learning objective.}
The analysis with the ``other'' Laplacians and the subsequent cross-comparison sharpens the picture considerably.
Maps trained with normalisation but evaluated unnormalised lose their alignment entirely, both to the topological and to the zero reference.
This deserves emphasis, because it constrains how the alignment of the previous paragraphs should be read.
One might suspect that local alignment with the normalised graph Laplacian indicates that the learnt restriction maps have become essentially identity-like.
If that were so, the same maps evaluated without normalisation would reproduce the unnormalised graph Laplacian, whose Forman profile vanishes identically, and we would therefore expect alignment with the zero reference.
This is not what we observe: the profile is far from zero.
The learnt maps are consequently \textbf{not} close to the identity, and the alignment we report is an effect mediated by the normalisation rather than evidence that the sheaf has collapsed onto a trivial configuration.
Interestingly, these maps do not show exactly the same behaviour as the maps trained in an unnormalised way, since they always show a pattern of increasing disalignment with respect to the zero reference that, as we saw earlier, was more nuanced when the maps are originally unnormalised.
Instead, the maps trained in an unnormalised way but evaluated with normalisation show a similar alignment with the topological reference, with a high correlation. Also the Wasserstein distance closely aligns with the topological reference. 
This also tells us, that while the unnormalized restriction maps are not the identity, they stilll align with a Laplacian that, when normalized, is close to the reference. 
This effect is non random, becase as we show at initialization the random sheaf does not show this alignment. 
In addition to this analysis, the cross-comparison gives a finer-grained picture of how the learning and normalisation effects are interlaced with each other.
The normalised maps show a moderately high correlation, and a Wasserstein distance that stays moderate to high and sustained across checkpoints, neither vanishing nor blowing up.
The RMSE and NRMSE instead highlight the difference between these representations, which is non-negligible and consistent across all the datasets.
The unnormalised cross-comparison shows the same sustained pattern: the correlation is generally low and the RMSE and NRMSE are rather high throughout training.
The corresponding global summaries are reported in Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:global_general_other_norm}, \ref{tab:global_general_other_unnorm}, \ref{tab:global_cross_a} and \ref{tab:global_cross_b}.
As an example, we consider the Pubmed dataset, whose results are shown in
Supplementary Figures~\ref{fig: Pubmed_Other_Norm}, ~\ref{fig: Pubmed_Other_Unnorm},
~\ref{fig: Pubmed_Cross_A} and ~\ref{fig: Pubmed_Cross_B}: the two \emph{other
Laplacian} (normalised and not normalised) profiles against their own references, and the two cross-comparisons along
the normalised and unnormalised columns.
\newline 
Taken together, this analysis serves as a form of \textbf{ablation study}: it establishes that the effects we observe in the Forman profile certainly depend on the normalisation, which plays a considerable role in how sheaf learning navigates the space, but also depend on the learning process of the chosen Sheaf Neural Network.

\paragraph{Joint diffusion produces a different signature.}

We compute the Forman profile also for the Joint Diffusion Sheaf process ~\cite{caralt2024jointdiffusionprocessesinductive}, which simulates an opinion dynamics model where both opinions and beliefs are adaptive and are modified towards a flexible equilibrium configuration.
Formally, the equation that governs this mechanism is $\begin{cases}
X(t+1) = X(t) - \sigma\big((\mathbf{I}_{nd} - \alpha \Delta_{\mathcal{F}}(t))(I_n \otimes W_1(t))XW_2(t)\big) \\
\mathcal{F}^T(t+1) = \mathcal{F}^T(t) - \sigma\big((\mathbf{I}_{2md} - \beta \Delta_{X(t)})(I_n \otimes W_1^T(t))\mathcal{F}^TW_2^T(t)\big)
\end{cases}$.
We tested it both in the unnormalised and in the normalised version, setting in both cases the initialisation of the restriction maps to the identity.
In this way, the Forman profile metrics also tell us how much the learnt sheaf deviates from the identity.
Unlike the standard Sheaf Neural Network, the unnormalised regime is presumably the standard one for this architecture.
In this modality, we observed that for very small datasets (Wisconsin, Cornell) the Joint Sheaf remains very close to the identity sheaf, as its Forman profile is essentially the same as that of the standard graph Laplacian. We show an example of this for the Wisconsin dataset in Figure~\ref{fig: Wisconsin_Joint_Unnorm} (Supplementary).
Instead, across all the bigger datasets there is a recurrent pattern: the maps deviate from the identity as one moves through the layers, but this disalignment is attenuated as the epochs increase. At random initialisation the sheaf therefore has a Forman profile very far from the identity, whereas as the epochs increase some disalignment remains but stays quite mild.
This is shown for the Questions dataset in Figure~\ref{fig: Questions_Joint_Unnorm} (Supplementary); the global pattern across all ten datasets is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:global_joint_unnorm}.

\paragraph{Discussion}

The results show that the Forman profile characterises each sheaf learning
mechanism and tracks how a Sheaf Neural Network \emph{navigates} the sheaf
space during training. This gives a first tool to observe, layer by layer and
epoch by epoch, how the learnt sheaf evolves and how far it departs from
specific reference sheaves, providing a more interpretable lens on a process
that is otherwise opaque.

Read together, our observations point to a concrete implicit bias of sheaf
learning: the normalised learning mechanism converges to a representation
whose Forman curvature is locally aligned with that of the standard
normalised graph Laplacian. This offers a concrete mechanism behind the
observation that identity sheaves often match learnt ones in accuracy
~\cite{caralt2026necessitylearnablesheaflaplacians}: the learnt sheaf is already drifting
towards that region of the sheaf space, so little expressive power is lost by
starting there.

The role of normalisation is clarified by the same data: a normalised sheaf
Laplacian is itself the sheaf Laplacian of another sheaf, so normalisation is
a move \emph{inside} the sheaf space rather than outside it. This suggests
that normalisation confines learning to a preferred subspace, and that the
operative constraint on sheaf learning is the \emph{navigability} of the
sheaf space rather than its size, as the mechanism-dependence below
illustrates.

We emphasise that the Forman profile behaves differently for each sheaf
learning mechanism we consider, which we read as a sort of
\textbf{fingerprint} of the process itself:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item normalised general sheaf learning converges to local alignment with
    the normalised graph Laplacian;
    \item unnormalised general sheaf learning aligns with neither reference:
    the metrics against the topological reference stay almost constant across
    layers and epochs, while the disalignment from the zero reference is large
    and generally grows, peaking at intermediate depth and then partially
    receding in the large heterophilic datasets;
    \item joint diffusion shows the reverse relationship with normalisation:
    in its unnormalised form it remains close to the identity on small
    datasets, and on larger ones drifts from it before the drift is
    attenuated with training, while its normalised counterpart instead departs
    from the identity increasingly as training proceeds.
\end{enumerate}
It is exactly this mechanism-dependence, the same optimiser producing three
distinct signatures under three training regimes, that a single, general
low-rank-solutions argument does not by itself explain.

The bias is dataset-dependent as well. A natural question is why Minesweeper,
and to a lesser extent Tolokers and Questions, depart from it. Node homophily
does not appear to be the deciding factor: Roman Empire is itself a strongly
heterophilic benchmark, yet its Forman
profile aligns with the topological reference as cleanly as the homophilic
datasets in our study. A more plausible candidate is that these are the only
binary classification tasks in our benchmark suite, so that the
feature-structure relationship the sheaf must encode may differ structurally
from the multi-class case; we leave a systematic test of this hypothesis to
future work. What the Minesweeper case does establish is that curvature
alignment is a tendency of the learning dynamics rather than a condition for
the sheaf to be predictive, since the model attains a strong AUC-ROC score
while departing from it entirely.

A recent, independent line of work interprets a related phenomenon,
oversmoothing in Sheaf Neural Networks, as a form of \emph{representation
degeneracy} ~\cite{donmez2026oversmoothingrepresentationdegeneracyneural}, arguing that the optimiser is drawn toward a
trivial subrepresentation of the learned sheaf. Both accounts describe an
implicit bias toward simple sheaf configurations, but the two differ in kind
rather than in degree: they diagnose a specific failure mode and correct it
architecturally, whereas we propose an observable that measures Laplacian
evolution without presupposing what a degenerate sheaf looks like. What we
find is accordingly softer, and our own data argue against reading it as
degeneracy: the same maps evaluated without normalisation do not approach the
zero profile, so they are not identity-like, and the cross-comparison shows
learnt sheaves remaining distinct from one another even as each aligns locally
with the same reference. We therefore read this as an inductive bias rather than a
degeneracy, and, as the two paragraphs above show, one that depends on both
the learning mechanism and the dataset. Their evidence, moreover, rests on
small graphs pushed to extreme depth to induce oversmoothing, while ours is
measured at ordinary depths on far larger datasets, where oversmoothing is not
yet the concern.

Beyond the specific findings, our results support reading the training
dynamics as a geometric flow. Because the sheaf is re-estimated at each layer from the current
features, the network is better described as a process that deforms the
geometry than as a diffusion with a fixed operator, in analogy with the Ricci
flow $\partial_t g = -2\,\mathrm{Ric}(g)$ ~\cite{Hamilton1982ThreemanifoldsWP}. An
analytic treatment of the sheaf flow is intractable in practice, but the
Forman profile gives a direct, computable measurement of it, tracking how the
sheaf Laplacian changes through the layers of the network and where it
converges.

To contextualise these results, we outline the main limitation of the
diagnostic: it captures only local characteristics of the sheaf. Each
curvature block sees only the neighbourhood of its node, and its eigenvalues
are computed in the stalk basis of that node, which differs from node to
node; long-range dependencies and global spectral structure are therefore
invisible to it. Local alignment with the graph Laplacian does not imply that
the learnt operator is globally equivalent to it, so the learnt sheaf may
still possess global, e.g.\ spectral, features to which the Forman profile is
blind. Even so, it is this locality that makes the profile cheap
enough to be tracked continuously, across ten datasets, three learning
mechanisms and every layer and checkpoint of training; a global spectral
descriptor would be a natural complement to it rather than a replacement.
